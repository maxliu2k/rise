\begin{abstract}
Instrument classification underpins Music Information Retrieval (MIR) infrastructure and supports applications including musicology, audio editing, and transcription. However, instrument recognition deep learning models may fail or lose substantial accuracy under artificial and real-world noise. This work benchmarks six instrument classification models---three trained from scratch (SVM, CNN, CRNN) and three pretrained (AST, MERT, and PANNs)---under varying forms and degrees of noise. We use the Philharmonia Orchestra Sound Samples dataset, trimmed to 12 common orchestral instruments. Noise was white (Gaussian), human non-speech from ESC-50, and environmental from DEMAND, at SNRs from 50 dB to $-$10 dB. All six models were scored on identical mixtures, regenerated from a fixed seed and verified by exact reproduction of committed per-window predictions, enabling paired comparison.

We find that our best clean (without noise) macro-F1 baselines are AST (0.991) and PANNs (0.989), followed by MERT (0.980), SVM (0.979), CRNN (0.974), and CNN (0.971). On clean audio all six models fell within two percentage points of one another, but robustness diverged sharply: at 20 dB SNR the SVM retained only 10.4\% of its clean macro-F1 under white noise against 68.0\% under human non-speech, and model ranking inverted---SVM fell from fourth on clean audio to last under white noise. Clean macro-F1 is therefore not indicative of noise robustness, and degradation depends on noise category, not severity alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Musical instruments are integral to the production and characterization of music, and their distinct timbral properties are intertwined with music composition, performance, and perception \cite{b1}. Consequently, automatically classifying instruments has become an active area of research, with applications in music transcription, source separation, post-production and automatic mixing, and music recommendation \cite{b2}.

Early instrument classification systems ($\sim$1999) relied on statistical classifiers such as Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) and Support Vector Machines (SVM) \cite{b3}. In the early 2000s, performance was improved with high-quality feature representation \cite{b4}, and also drew attention to the difficulties of real-world recordings \cite{b5}. From around 2015, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) transformed instrument classification, and subsequent works pushed accuracy and precision past 95\% on clean, isolated-note datasets \cite{b6,b7,b8}.

High accuracy on clean instrument audio reveals little about performance under perturbation; controlled degradation probes that relationship directly. Other instrument classification research shifts towards realism, such as polyphonic recognition \cite{b9}, whereas noise-robust classification is much less explored. It is therefore pertinent to establish not only which classification systems fail under noise, but which instruments they confuse and which noise categories cause it. Where robustness analysis has been done, model span is narrow. Deng et al. \cite{b10} studied the pretrained models OpenL3, PANNs, and CLAP under parameterized audio effects (gain, low-pass filtering, reverberation, and bitcrushing), finding that pretraining does not automatically confer robustness; additive noise, the perturbation class studied here, was not covered. Elsewhere, comparisons similarly span only a single model family, such as pretrained embeddings \cite{b11}. No prior instrument classification study was identified that spans handcrafted features, models trained from scratch, and pretrained backbones. This breadth is deliberate but carries a trade-off: pretraining corpora, input representation, sample rate, and architecture all differ, so family-level differences cannot be attributed to a single factor. Complete classification systems are therefore compared, and performance differences are not attributed to architecture alone.

The research question is: how do instrument classification systems differ in their ability to retain classification performance under white, human, and environmental additive noise across a range of signal-to-noise ratios? The contributions are: (i) a matched robustness benchmark of six model families spanning handcrafted, scratch-trained, and pretrained approaches; (ii) an evaluation protocol in which every model is scored on the same generated noisy mixtures, enabling paired comparison; and (iii) per-instrument and per-noise degradation analysis, including a pre-registered test of whether acoustically similar instruments are confused more heavily under noise.


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Materials}
The Philharmonia Orchestra's sound samples dataset was used \cite{b12}, which comprises thousands of samples across over 20 instruments. Specifically, 12 common orchestral instruments were analyzed---bassoon, cello, clarinet, double bass, flute, french horn, oboe, trombone, trumpet, tuba, viola, and violin. Instruments averaged 45.3 distinct pitches. Class imbalance ranges from 433 (trumpet) to 852 (violin), a ratio of 1.97:1. Exactly one articulation was retained per instrument---\textit{normal} for winds and brass, \textit{arco-normal} for the four bowed strings---so that playing technique could not vary with class, and four recordings carrying conflicting labels were excluded. Ultimately, the dataset had 8,374 unique recordings. For human non-speech and environmental noise, the ESC-50 \cite{b13} and DEMAND \cite{b14} datasets were used.

\subsection{Data Pre-Processing \& Splitting}
\subsubsection{Pre-Processing}
Leading and trailing silence more than 30 dB below each recording's peak was trimmed. One 3.0-second window was created per source recording, tiling (repeating) all recordings under 3.0 seconds. Audio was resampled to 22.05 kHz. This rate is not arbitrary: MP3 bitrate in the source archive varies by instrument family (64, 80, and 96 kbps), so at 44.1 kHz the codec's brick-wall cutoff falls below Nyquist and becomes a per-class shortcut available to any model. At 22.05 kHz the cutoff lies above Nyquist and is discarded; the pipeline asserts this for every resampled file. Loudness was normalized by scaling each window towards an RMS amplitude of 0.1, with gain capped to keep amplitude below 0.99.
\subsubsection{Splitting}
Recordings were split by instrument-pitch group, targeting 70/15/15 train, validation, and test, so that performances of the same note at different dynamics could not appear in more than one split. The split was deterministic, yielding 5,861 training, 1,258 validation, and 1,255 test windows across 544 pitch groups, and was reused by all models.

Six models spanning a range of learning methods are compared. Because architectures differ substantially, each was trained with its native recipe (Table~\ref{tab:model_configs}). Weights were fitted on the training split; hyperparameters and checkpoints were selected on validation only.

\textbf{SVM.} Frame-wise acoustic features were summarized by their
temporal means and standard deviations into 88 dimensions per window,
normalized using training-set statistics. An RBF kernel was selected by
validation macro-F1.
\textbf{MERT.} MERT-v1-95M \cite{b15} returns 13 hidden-state
representations, each time-averaged, combined by learned layer weights
and passed to a 12-class linear head. The backbone and head were
fine-tuned jointly.
\textbf{AST.} Initialized from
MIT/ast-finetuned-audioset-10-10-0.4593 \cite{b16}; Log-Mel spectrograms,
backbone and head fine-tuned jointly.
\textbf{PANNs.} AudioSet-pretrained CNN14 \cite{b17} computes 64-band
log-Mel internally. The backbone was fine-tuned and its classifier
replaced by a 12-way head over a 2048-dimensional embedding.
\textbf{CNN.} Standardized 128-band log-Mel spectrograms were processed
by three convolutional blocks of 32, 64, and 128 channels
($3\times3$ convolution, batch normalization, ReLU, $2\times2$ max
pooling; $\sim$111{,}000 parameters).
\textbf{CRNN.} The same blocks as CNN, except the final one pools only on
frequency. Frequency is then collapsed to a 32-step sequence of
128-dimensional vectors read by a bidirectional GRU (128 units per
direction), time-averaged and passed to a linear classifier
($\sim$294{,}000 parameters).

Neural model training used validation-based checkpoint selection, early stopping, dropout, weight decay, and pretrained initialization where applicable. CNN and CRNN predictions were ensembled across 5 seeds (42--46).


\subsection{Noise Construction}
All models were evaluated on the same mixtures. Three noise categories were used: white (Gaussian, spectrally flat), human non-speech, and environmental. Human non-speech noises were drawn from ESC-50 targets 20--29. Environmental noise was drawn from all 18 DEMAND environments, spanning domestic, nature, office, public, street, and transportation settings. For each ESC-50 and DEMAND realization, one source clip or environment was drawn at random and one random three-second segment extracted, rejecting near-silent extracts, then resampled to 22.05 kHz.

Let $x$ denote a clean waveform and $n$ a selected noise waveform, each containing $N$ samples, and let $P(z)=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_i^{2}$ denote mean-squared waveform power. The noise waveform was first centered by subtracting its mean:
\begin{equation}
\bar{n} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_i,
\qquad
n_{c,i} = n_i-\bar{n},
\label{eq:noise_centering}
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ is the centered noise waveform. For a target signal-to-noise ratio $s$ in decibels, the centered noise was multiplied by gain
\begin{equation}
\alpha =
\sqrt{
\frac{P(x)}
{P(n_c)\,10^{s/10}}
}.
\label{eq:noise_gain}
\end{equation}
The noisy mixture was then constructed as
\begin{equation}
y_i = x_i+\alpha n_{c,i},
\label{eq:noise_mixture}
\end{equation}
where $y$ is the final noisy waveform.

Mixtures were generated at eight SNRs (50, 40, 30, 20, 10, 0, $-5$, and $-10$ dB). Two independently seeded noise draws were generated for each of the 1,255 test recordings and noise categories, producing 60,240 noisy WAV files in total ($1{,}255 \times 3\,\text{ noise categories} \times 8\,\text{ SNRs} \times 2\,\text{ realizations}$). Within each replicate, a single realization was reused and rescaled across all eight SNRs, so change along a robustness curve could be attributed to noise level instead of changing noise. Every model was scored on the same realizations, allowing paired comparison.

Each realization was seeded deterministically from the dataset identity, window, noise category and replicate index, making the corpus reproducible; regeneration was verified by reproducing a previously scored model's committed per-window predictions exactly, for every noise category. After writing, each mixture was reloaded and required to match the target SNR within 0.1 dB, with residual DC below 1\% of the added-noise power. Mixtures were stored as 32-bit floats without clipping or post-mixing renormalization. Low SNRs can exceed the $[-1, 1]$ range, but clipping is non-linear and would corrupt the intended power ratio.

These are nominal full-band SNRs. Spectral diagnostics were recorded over 25 Hz--8 kHz, but the three categories were not equalized for in-band power, so differences between categories partly reflect their differing spectral distributions and should not be interpreted as differences in perceptual harmfulness alone.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\subsubsection{Primary Metrics}

Each three-second window was one example; the pipeline retained one window per source recording, so no recording received extra weight. Macro-F1 was the primary metric: F1 is computed per instrument and averaged, giving every instrument equal weight regardless of class imbalance. For noise evaluation, macro-F1 was calculated separately for each model, noise category, SNR, and realization.

Clean performance retained was defined as the proportion of clean macro-F1 remaining under a noisy condition:
\begin{equation}
Q_{m,n,s,r}
=
\frac{
F_{m,n,s,r}
}{
F_{m,\mathrm{clean}}
},
\label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
where \(F_{m,n,s,r}\) is the macro-F1 of model \(m\) under noise category \(n\), SNR \(s\), and realization \(r\), and \(F_{m,\mathrm{clean}}\) is that model's clean macro-F1. A value of \(1\) indicates no degradation; \(0.5\) indicates half the clean macro-F1 retained. The two realization scores were averaged at each noise-category--SNR point, giving \(\overline{Q}_{m,n,s}=(Q_{m,n,s,0}+Q_{m,n,s,1})/2\).

Overall robustness was summarized using the normalized area under the retention-versus-SNR curve. For ascending SNR values $s_1<\cdots<s_J$, normalized AUC was calculated using the trapezoidal rule:
\begin{equation}
A_{m,n}
=
\frac{1}{s_J-s_1}
\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}
\left(s_{j+1}-s_j\right)
\frac{
\overline{Q}_{m,n,s_j}
+
\overline{Q}_{m,n,s_{j+1}}
}{2}.
\label{eq:retention_auc}
\end{equation}
An AUC of $1$ indicates that clean macro-F1 was retained across the complete SNR range. Paired model comparisons were matched by test window, noise category, SNR, and realization, and reported as macro-F1 of model $A$ minus model $B$.


\subsubsection{Secondary Failure Analysis}

CNN and CRNN were compared at 20 dB under white, human non-speech, and environmental noise, with the two realizations analyzed separately. Differences are CNN macro-F1 minus CRNN macro-F1, and positive values favor CNN. Uncertainty was estimated with 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of complete instrument-pitch groups, drawn with replacement, retaining every test window belonging to each selected group. The same resampled groups were evaluated by both models, and the difference recalculated for each resample. The 95\% percentile-bootstrap interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled differences. Associations between recall loss and five training-derived acoustic summaries were evaluated using Spearman correlations with 100,000 instrument-label permutations. Recall loss was calculated separately for each model, instrument, noise category, SNR, and realization as clean recall minus noisy recall. These associations were exploratory, not confirmatory tests.

To examine whether acoustically similar instruments became confused under noise, each instrument was represented by the mean of its standardized 88-dimensional training-set feature vectors, and acoustic distance between two instruments as the Euclidean distance between these mean vectors. Symmetric confusion for a pair is the mean of the two row-normalized error rates between them. Clean pairwise confusion was subtracted from each noisy value, and the resulting noise-induced confusion was summarized across the complete SNR range using normalized curve area. Spearman correlation related acoustic distance to noise-induced confusion for each model-noise pair. The two realization-specific curve areas were averaged as $\overline{A}_{m,a,b,n}^{\mathrm{conf}}$ for testing, giving the observed relationship
\begin{equation}
\rho_{m,n}
=
\operatorname{Spearman}
\left(
\left\{D_{a,b}\right\},
\left\{\overline{A}_{m,a,b,n}^{\mathrm{conf}}\right\}
\right),
\qquad a<b.
\label{eq:distance_confusion_correlation}
\end{equation}
Statistical significance was estimated using 100,000 permutations in which complete instrument identities were permuted together across matrix rows and columns, preserving dependencies among the 66 instrument pairs. Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) correction was applied across the declared family of 18 comparisons (six models $\times$ three noise categories) at $q<0.05$.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Clean Classification Performance}
Clean audio provided little separation among the systems. Macro-F1 spanned
2.0 points across all six models (Table~\ref{tab:auc}), from 0.9708 (CNN)
to 0.9908 (AST), which also achieved the highest clean accuracy (0.9912).
Accuracy and macro-F1 differed by no more than 0.0021 for any model.
Because SVM, AST, MERT, and PANNs were evaluated from single fitted runs,
these small clean-score differences were not tested against training-seed
variance; CNN and CRNN are five-seed soft-vote ensembles whose single-seed
test macro-F1 was $0.962 \pm 0.021$ and $0.973 \pm 0.003$ respectively.

\subsection{Noise Robustness}
AST had the highest retention AUC under all three noise categories
(Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}, Table~\ref{tab:auc}). At 20 dB under white
noise AST retained 72.3\% of its clean macro-F1 and SVM 10.4\%, a
61.9-point difference and the largest observed between any two models at a
shared condition. Across the full white-noise curve AST reached a retention
AUC of 0.636 and MERT 0.573, while SVM ranked last at 0.259.

The systems degraded less severely under recorded noise at the same nominal
SNR. At 20 dB human non-speech noise, AST, MERT, and PANNs retained 85.3\%,
84.6\%, and 83.6\% respectively; over the full curve PANNs exceeded MERT
(AUC 0.778 against 0.755) while AST remained highest at 0.791. Under
environmental noise at 20 dB, AST retained 83.2\% and achieved an AUC of
0.753, followed by PANNs at 81.2\% and 0.726. CNN was least robust under
both recorded categories, retaining 46.4\% under environmental noise for an
AUC of 0.505. The separation appears well before the noise is perceptually
meaningful: at 50 dB SNR, CNN has already lost 16.0\% of its clean macro-F1
and CRNN 13.9\%, while AST retains 99.1\% and MERT 97.8\%.

\subsection{Paired Comparisons}
Comparisons were window- and realization-matched, and every window was
verified to have been scored against the same noise realization by both
models. At 20 dB white noise, CNN exceeded CRNN by 0.1122 macro-F1 in the
first realization and 0.1130 in the second; the 95\% pitch-group bootstrap
intervals $[0.0560, 0.1625]$ and $[0.0589, 0.1601]$ both excluded zero.
Under human non-speech and environmental noise CRNN was better, with
intervals excluding zero in one realization but not the other. Four of the
six intervals excluded zero, including both white-noise intervals. None of
the six exact cluster sign tests remained significant after
Benjamini--Hochberg correction.

\subsection{Instrument-Specific Failures}
Averaged across models and noise categories, tuba lost the most recall
(Fig.~\ref{fig:recall_loss}, AUC 0.599) and violin the least (0.215). The
pattern was partly model-specific: tuba was the most vulnerable instrument
for five of six models under human non-speech noise, but the leading failure
class differed under white and environmental noise. The largest single
pairwise increase was flute-oboe for the SVM under white noise (0.342).
Acoustically closer instrument pairs tended to show greater noise-induced
confusion (Fig.~\ref{fig:distance_confusion}): the association was negative
in 17 of the 18 predeclared model-by-noise tests, and seven were significant
after Benjamini--Hochberg correction, most strongly for AST under human
non-speech noise ($\rho = -0.607$, permutation $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}$,
adjusted $q = 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$). Six of the seven significant tests
involved recorded rather than synthetic noise.

\section{Discussion}
Results show that clean performance is not indicative of robustness under noise. While the models' macro-F1 and accuracy ratings conformed closely with each other when tested on unperturbed audio, their performances diverged sharply under noise, and model ranking changed across both noise categories and noise levels. AST was the most accurate and the most robust throughout, while SVM lost the most performance under white noise and CNN under environmental noise. One key takeaway is that clean-trained AST is most noise robust under the conditions evaluated in this study, but these results cannot substantiate conclusions of model performance under noise-aware training. There could plausibly be a correlation, but future work is necessary.

\subsection{Limitations}
First, the Philharmonia dataset contains only single-instrument,
single-note recordings. 97.3\% of recordings were shorter than three seconds and were tiled to fill the window, which could confound content with repetition. This was probed directly: loop period alone predicts instrument at 0.184 balanced accuracy against a chance level of 0.083, and the energy envelope alone at 0.419, so the shortcut is available, but a trained model's misclassifications favour period-matched classes no more often than a five-seed baseline (0.4538 against 0.4592, range 0.3844--0.5254). The confound therefore appears unused rather than absent, and that test rests on 26 errors, so it rules out only a large effect. Second, the noise corpus does not cover recording or studio effects such as reverberation. Third, because of time and compute constraints only two noise realizations were generated, giving a sensitivity check but not a reliable variance estimate. Fourth, nominal SNR is full-band, and the categories concentrate energy differently across frequency, so they may mask instruments unequally, making direct comparison of noise loudness unattainable. Lastly, models were trained on clean data only, so this study measures robustness without any noise adaptation.

\section{Conclusion}
This research compares and ranks six musical-instrument classification systems under controlled white, human non-speech, and environmental noise degradation. Despite similar performance on clean recordings, AST retained the most clean macro-F1 across the tested conditions, while SVM degraded fastest under white noise and CNN under environmental. Failures were also uneven across instruments: tuba, oboe, and trumpet showed the greatest recall loss, and acoustically similar instrument pairs tended to be confused more heavily under noise---the association was negative in 17 of 18 model-by-noise tests and significant in seven after correction, six of them under recorded noise. These findings demonstrate that clean performance alone is insufficient for selecting an instrument classifier for noisy conditions; robustness must also be evaluated across multiple noise categories and severities. Future work should extend this benchmark to multiple datasets, polyphonic music, independently recorded environmental noise, additional noise realizations, and noise-aware training.
