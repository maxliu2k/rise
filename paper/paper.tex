\begin{abstract}
Instrument classification underpins Music Information Retrieval (MIR) infrastructure and supports applications including musicology, audio editing, and transcription. Deep learning models that perform well on clean recordings may lose substantial accuracy under artificial and real-world noise, yet robustness is rarely compared across model families. We construct a reproducible noise-robustness benchmark for instrument classification---12 orchestral instruments from the Philharmonia Orchestra Sound Samples dataset, perturbed by white (Gaussian), human non-speech (ESC-50), and environmental (DEMAND) noise at SNRs from 50 dB to $-$10 dB---and evaluate six systems on it, three trained from scratch (SVM, CNN, CRNN) and three pretrained (AST, MERT, and PANNs). Every mixture is generated from a fixed seed and regenerates exactly, so all six are scored on identical audio and every comparison is paired. Clean macro-F1 spanned only 0.971 to 0.991 across the six, but robustness diverged sharply. Averaged over the SNR range, the models retained 26--64\% of their clean macro-F1 under white noise and 65--79\% under human non-speech; the ranges do not overlap, so no choice of model compensated for the change in noise category. AST was the most robust under all three categories and the only model to hold its rank across them. Clean macro-F1 is therefore not indicative of noise robustness, and degradation depends on noise category at the same nominal full-band SNR, not severity alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Musical instruments are integral to the production and characterization of music, and their distinct timbral properties are intertwined with music composition, performance, and perception \cite{b1}. Consequently, automatically classifying instruments has become an active area of research, with applications in music transcription, source separation, post-production and automatic mixing, and music recommendation \cite{b2}. Early systems (from the late 1990s) relied on statistical classifiers \cite{b3}, and performance was later improved with high-quality feature representations \cite{b4}. The difficulties of real-world recordings were also acknowledged during this period \cite{b5}. From around 2015, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) pushed accuracy and precision past 95\% on clean, isolated-note datasets \cite{b6,b7,b8}.

High accuracy on clean instrument audio reveals little about performance under perturbation; controlled degradation probes that relationship directly. Other instrument classification research shifts towards realism, such as polyphonic recognition \cite{b9}, whereas noise-robust classification is much less explored. Where robustness analysis has been done, model span is narrow. Deng et al. \cite{b10} showed that pretrained OpenL3, PANNs, and CLAP embeddings remained sensitive to parameterized audio effects---gain, low-pass filtering, reverberation, and bitcrushing---but additive noise, the perturbation class studied here, was not evaluated. No prior instrument classification study was identified that spans handcrafted features, models trained from scratch, and pretrained backbones. This breadth is deliberate but carries a trade-off: pretraining corpora, input representation, sample rate, and architecture all differ, so family-level differences cannot be attributed to a single factor. Complete classification systems are therefore compared, and performance differences are not attributed to architecture alone.

The research question is: how do instrument classification systems differ in their ability to retain classification performance under white, human, and environmental additive noise across a range of signal-to-noise ratios? The contributions are: (i) a reproducible noise-robustness benchmark for instrument classification---60,240 deterministically seeded mixtures spanning three noise categories and eight SNRs, regenerable exactly from the scheme in Section~\ref{sec:noise}; (ii) an evaluation of six systems on that benchmark, spanning handcrafted, scratch-trained, and pretrained approaches, every one scored on identical mixtures so that all comparisons are paired; and (iii) per-instrument and per-noise degradation analysis, including a pre-defined test of whether acoustically similar instruments are confused more heavily under noise.


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Materials}
The Philharmonia Orchestra's sound samples dataset was used \cite{b11}, which comprises thousands of samples across over 20 instruments. Specifically, 12 common orchestral instruments were analyzed---bassoon, cello, clarinet, double bass, flute, french horn, oboe, trombone, trumpet, tuba, viola, and violin. Instruments averaged 45.3 distinct pitches. Class imbalance ranges from 433 (trumpet) to 852 (violin), a ratio of 1.97:1. Exactly one articulation was retained per instrument---\textit{normal} for winds and brass, \textit{arco-normal} for the four bowed strings---so that playing technique could not vary with class, and four recordings carrying conflicting labels were excluded. Ultimately, the dataset had 8,374 unique recordings. For human non-speech and environmental noise, the ESC-50 \cite{b12} and DEMAND \cite{b13} datasets were used.

\subsection{Data Pre-Processing \& Splitting}
\subsubsection{Pre-Processing}
Leading and trailing silence more than 30 dB below each recording's peak was trimmed. One 3.0-second window was created per source recording, tiling (repeating) all recordings under 3.0 seconds. Audio was resampled to 22.05 kHz. This rate is not arbitrary: MP3 bitrate in the source archive varies by instrument family (64, 80, and 96 kbps), so at 44.1 kHz the codec's brick-wall cutoff falls below Nyquist and becomes a per-class shortcut available to any model. At 22.05 kHz the cutoff lies above Nyquist and is discarded; the pipeline asserts this for every resampled file. Loudness was normalized by scaling each window towards an RMS amplitude of 0.1, with gain capped to keep amplitude below 0.99.
\subsubsection{Splitting}
Recordings were split by instrument-pitch group, targeting 70/15/15 train, validation, and test, so that performances of the same note at different dynamics could not appear in more than one split. The split was deterministic, yielding 5,861 training, 1,258 validation, and 1,255 test windows across 544 pitch groups, and was reused by all models.

Because architectures differ substantially, each was trained with its native recipe (Table~\ref{tab:model_configs}). Weights were fitted on the training split; hyperparameters and checkpoints were selected on validation only.

The SVM used standardized 88-dimensional acoustic summaries. CNN and CRNN learned from log-Mel spectrograms, with CRNN adding bidirectional recurrent processing. MERT \cite{b14}, AST \cite{b15}, and PANNs \cite{b16} were fine-tuned from pretrained backbones with new 12-class heads. Neural model training used validation-based checkpoint selection, early stopping, dropout, weight decay, and pretrained initialization where applicable. CNN and CRNN predictions were ensembled across 5 seeds (42--46).


\subsection{Noise Construction}
\label{sec:noise}
All models were evaluated on the same mixtures. Three noise categories were used: white (Gaussian, spectrally flat), human non-speech, and environmental. Human non-speech noises were drawn from ESC-50 targets 20--29. Environmental noise was drawn from all 18 DEMAND environments, spanning domestic, nature, office, public, street, and transportation settings. For each ESC-50 and DEMAND realization, one source clip or environment was drawn at random and one random three-second segment extracted, rejecting near-silent extracts, then resampled to 22.05 kHz.

Let $x$ denote a clean waveform and $n$ a selected noise waveform, each containing $N$ samples, and let $P(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_i^{2}$ denote mean-squared waveform power. The noise waveform was first centered by subtracting its mean:
\begin{equation}
\bar{n} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_i,
\qquad
n_{c,i} = n_i-\bar{n},
\label{eq:noise_centering}
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ is the centered noise waveform. For a target signal-to-noise ratio $s$ in decibels, the centered noise was multiplied by gain
\begin{equation}
\alpha =
\sqrt{
\frac{P(x)}
{P(n_c)\,10^{s/10}}
}.
\label{eq:noise_gain}
\end{equation}
The noisy mixture was then constructed as
\begin{equation}
y_i = x_i+\alpha n_{c,i},
\label{eq:noise_mixture}
\end{equation}
where $y$ is the final noisy waveform.

Mixtures were generated at eight SNRs (50, 40, 30, 20, 10, 0, $-5$, and $-10$ dB). Two independently seeded noise draws were generated for each of the 1,255 test recordings and noise categories, producing 60,240 noisy WAV files in total ($1{,}255 \times 3\,\text{ noise categories} \times 8\,\text{ SNRs} \times 2\,\text{ realizations}$). Within each replicate, a single realization was reused and rescaled across all eight SNRs, so change along a robustness curve could be attributed to noise level instead of changing noise. Every model was scored on the same realizations, allowing paired comparison.

Each realization was seeded deterministically from the dataset identity, window, noise category and replicate index, making the corpus reproducible; regeneration was verified by reproducing a previously scored model's committed per-window predictions exactly, for every noise category. After writing, each mixture was reloaded and required to match the target SNR within 0.1 dB, with residual DC below 1\% of the added-noise power. Mixtures were stored as 32-bit floats without clipping or post-mixing renormalization. Low SNRs can exceed the $[-1, 1]$ range, but clipping is non-linear and would corrupt the intended power ratio.

These are nominal full-band SNRs. Spectral diagnostics were recorded over 25 Hz--8 kHz, but the three categories were not equalized for in-band power, so differences between categories partly reflect their differing spectral distributions and should not be interpreted as differences in perceptual harmfulness alone.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\subsubsection{Primary Metrics}

Each three-second window was one example; the pipeline retained one window per source recording, so no recording received extra weight. Macro-F1 was the primary metric: F1 is computed per instrument and averaged, giving every instrument equal weight regardless of class imbalance. For noise evaluation, macro-F1 was calculated separately for each model, noise category, SNR, and realization.

Clean performance retained was defined as the proportion of clean macro-F1 remaining under a noisy condition:
\begin{equation}
Q_{m,n,s,r}
=
\frac{
F_{m,n,s,r}
}{
F_{m,\mathrm{clean}}
},
\label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
where \(F_{m,n,s,r}\) is the macro-F1 of model \(m\) under noise category \(n\), SNR \(s\), and realization \(r\), and \(F_{m,\mathrm{clean}}\) is that model's clean macro-F1. A value of \(1\) indicates no degradation; \(0.5\) indicates half the clean macro-F1 retained. The two realization scores were averaged at each noise-category--SNR point, giving \(\overline{Q}_{m,n,s}=(Q_{m,n,s,0}+Q_{m,n,s,1})/2\).

Overall robustness was summarized using the normalized area under the retention-versus-SNR curve. For ascending SNR values $s_1<\cdots<s_J$, normalized AUC was calculated using the trapezoidal rule:
\begin{equation}
A_{m,n}
=
\frac{1}{s_J-s_1}
\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}
\left(s_{j+1}-s_j\right)
\frac{
\overline{Q}_{m,n,s_j}
+
\overline{Q}_{m,n,s_{j+1}}
}{2}.
\label{eq:retention_auc}
\end{equation}
Paired model comparisons were matched by test window, noise category, SNR, and realization, and reported as macro-F1 of model $A$ minus model $B$.


\subsubsection{Secondary Failure Analysis}

CNN and CRNN were compared at 20 dB under white, human non-speech, and environmental noise, with the two realizations analyzed separately. Differences are CNN macro-F1 minus CRNN macro-F1, and positive values favor CNN. Uncertainty was estimated with 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of complete instrument-pitch groups, drawn with replacement, retaining every test window belonging to each selected group. The same resampled groups were evaluated by both models, and the difference recalculated for each resample. The 95\% percentile-bootstrap interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled differences. No multiplicity correction was applied to the bootstrap intervals.

Instrument failure was measured as clean recall minus noisy recall for each model, instrument, noise category, SNR, and realization. Recall loss was summarized across SNR using normalized AUC and then averaged across the two realizations. To examine whether acoustically similar instruments became confused under noise, each instrument was represented by the centroid of its standardized 88-dimensional training-set feature vectors, and acoustic distance between two instruments was the Euclidean distance between those centroids. Symmetric confusion for a pair is the mean of the two row-normalized error rates between them. Clean pairwise confusion was subtracted from each noisy value, and the resulting noise-induced confusion was summarized across the complete SNR range using normalized curve area, then averaged across the two realizations. For each of the 18 model-noise combinations (six models $\times$ three noise categories), Spearman correlation related acoustic distance to noise-induced confusion across all 66 instrument pairs. Statistical significance was estimated using 100,000 permutations of complete instrument identities across matrix rows and columns. Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) correction was applied across the declared family of 18 comparisons at $q<0.05$.

\subsection{Noise Robustness}
AST had the highest retention AUC under all three noise categories (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}, Table~\ref{tab:auc}). Under 20 dB white noise, AST retained 72.3\% of its clean macro-F1 and SVM 10.4\%, a 61.9-point difference and the largest observed between any two models at a shared condition. Other rankings varied. At 20 dB, changing the noise realization altered retention by at most 3.6 points, compared with a 61.9-point maximum difference between models.

\subsection{Instrument-Specific Failures}
Averaged across models and noise categories, tuba lost the most recall
(Fig.~\ref{fig:recall_loss}, AUC 0.599) and violin the least (0.215). The pattern was partly model-specific: tuba was the most vulnerable instrument for five of six models under human non-speech noise, but the leading failure class differed under white and environmental noise. The largest single pairwise increase was flute-oboe for the SVM under white noise (0.342). Acoustically closer instrument pairs tended to show greater noise-induced confusion (Fig.~\ref{fig:distance_confusion}A): the association was negative in 17 of the 18 predeclared model-by-noise tests, and seven were significant after BH correction, most strongly for AST (Fig.~\ref{fig:distance_confusion}B) under human

\section{Discussion}
Results show that clean accuracy is not indicative of noise robustness. While the six models scored closely on clean audio, their performances were very different under noise, exposing differences in retention up to 61.9 percentage points at a shared noisy condition. Model ranking also changed across noise types and noise levels. Despite pretrained models having the highest retention AUCs under all noise categories, the study cannot establish that pretraining confers robustness because complete systems differed in pretraining corpus, architecture, input representation, and sample rate. For example, AST and MERT may have benefited from representations learned through large-scale audio pretraining, but PANNs trailed both under white noise, so the size of the benefit appears to depend on the pretraining corpus and architecture rather than on pretraining alone. The split was nonetheless consistent: in every noise category the three pretrained models ranked ahead of all three trained from scratch.

Human non-speech and environmental noise appeared less damaging than white noise, but this result could reflect limitations regarding the use of nominal SNR (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The only architectural comparison was between the CNN and CRNN, where results suggest that temporal sequence modeling was not uniformly beneficial: CNN led under white noise, with both confidence intervals excluding zero, while CRNN led under recorded noise, though only two of its four intervals excluded zero. This nuance suggests that the value of temporal information depends on noise structure, but still cannot be solely attributed to recurrence given that CRNN had more parameters and greater temporal resolution.

Robustness varied by instrument: tuba showed the greatest overall recall loss, whereas violin showed the least. However, the most vulnerable instrument still varied across models and noise categories, suggesting instrument failure depends on a combination of instrument-specific properties, noise, and model representation. Acoustic distance offered a partial answer: closer pairs were associated with more noise-induced confusion in 17 of the 18 model-noise tests, which could explain the interdependency between noise and instrument properties, as noise could mask distinguishing cues. The direction was therefore near-unanimous, but only seven relationships reached significance, so acoustic similarity does not explain failures consistently across all models and noise categories.



\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
First, the Philharmonia dataset contains only single-instrument, single-note recordings. 97.3\% of recordings were under three seconds and were tiled to fill the window, so the loop rate encodes note length, which itself correlates with instrument. This correlation has noticeable effects, as a classifier given only the loop period reaches 0.184 balanced accuracy against 0.083 for chance. The models do not appear to use it, however, since the order-sensitive CRNN confused similar-length instruments no more often than the CNN. Second, the noise corpus does not cover recording or studio effects such as reverberation or codec artifacts, which must be explored further. Third, because of time and compute constraints only two noise realizations were generated, giving a sensitivity check but not a reliable variance estimate. Fourth, nominal SNR is full-band, and the categories concentrate energy differently across frequency, so they may mask instruments unequally; comparisons between noise categories at a matched nominal SNR therefore bound what can be claimed about the noise itself, though comparisons between models are unaffected because every model was scored on identical mixtures. Lastly, models were trained on clean data only, so this study measures robustness without any noise adaptation.

\section{Conclusion}
This research contributes a reproducible noise-robustness benchmark for instrument classification and uses it to compare and rank six systems under controlled white, human non-speech, and environmental noise degradation. Despite similar performance on clean recordings, AST retained the most clean macro-F1 across the tested conditions, while SVM degraded fastest under white noise and CNN under environmental. Failures were also uneven across instruments: tuba, oboe, and trumpet showed the greatest recall loss, and acoustically similar instrument pairs tended to be confused more heavily under noise---the association was negative in 17 of 18 model-by-noise tests and significant in seven after correction, six of them under recorded noise. These findings demonstrate that clean performance alone is insufficient for selecting an instrument classifier for noisy conditions; robustness must also be evaluated across multiple noise categories and severities. Because the corpus regenerates exactly from its seeding scheme, further systems can be added and compared against these results directly. Future work should extend the benchmark to multiple datasets, polyphonic music, independently recorded environmental noise, additional noise realizations, and noise-aware training.
